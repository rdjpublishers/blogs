Google Search Operators: A Practical Cheat Sheet

Google has dozens of little keywords that turn it from a search engine into a precision tool. Most people know quotation marks for exact phrases. Almost nobody knows the rest. Here are the ones that actually save you time, with copy-paste examples for each.

By RDJ Publishers · 28 August 2026 · 8 min read

This is not an exhaustive list. Google's full operator set runs to fifty-plus keywords. The twelve below are the ones that pay for themselves the first time you use them. Each example is real — paste it into Google and see what comes back.

1. "exact phrase"

"two word phrase"

Match the exact phrase, in this order, with no words in between.

Use when: you know the exact words that should appear together. Default Google search matches words in any order, so quotes fix that.

"speed up chrome"

2. site:

site:example.com

Restrict results to a single domain. Combine with other terms to search within a site.

Use when: searching a specific site (especially one with a poor built-in search). Works for whole domains or subdomains.

site:rdjpublishers.com python

3. filetype:

filetype:pdf

Return only files of a specific type. Common values: pdf, doc, docx, xls, ppt, csv, json, txt.

Use when: looking for a downloadable document, a specific data format, or just to skip HTML results.

python pandas tutorial filetype:pdf

4. intitle:

intitle:keyword

Match pages whose title contains the keyword (not the body).

Use when: you want articles specifically about the topic, not pages that just mention it in passing.

intitle:python intitle:projects intitle:beginners

5. inurl:

inurl:keyword

Match pages whose URL contains the keyword.

Use when: looking for pages of a specific kind (the URL hints at the structure).

inurl:admin inurl:login

6. intext:

intext:keyword

Match pages whose body text contains the keyword, ignoring the title and URL.

Use when: combined with intitle: to find pages that are highly focused on a topic.

intitle:python intext:"for beginners"

7. - (minus)

keyword -excluded

Exclude results containing the term after the minus sign.

Use when: a word has multiple meanings and you want to filter out the irrelevant one.

python -snake -monty

8. OR

keyword1 OR keyword2

Match either keyword. Must be uppercase.

Use when: searching for two related concepts where you don't care which one appears.

chrome OR firefox OR edge slow

9. * (wildcard)

"the * is"

Stand-in for any word or phrase.

Use when: you remember part of a phrase but not all of it. Combine with quotes.

"the * is mightier than the sword"

10. before: and after:

before:2026-01-01

Restrict results to before or after a date (YYYY-MM-DD).

Use when: filtering out stale results, finding announcements within a window, or researching how a topic was covered in a specific period.

site:github.com python tutorial after:2025-01-01

11. cache:

cache:example.com/article

Show Google's cached version of a page.

Use when: a page is down or has changed, and you want to see what Google last saw. Less reliable in 2026 than it used to be, but still works sometimes.

cache:rdjpublishers.com/blogs/

12. related:

related:example.com

Find sites that Google considers similar.

Use when: discovering competitors or alternative resources on a topic.

related:reddit.com

Combining operators

The real power is chaining them. A few patterns that solve common problems:

Find a downloadable PDF guide on a topic, hosted on a trusted site:

python tutorial filetype:pdf (site:realpython.com OR site:docs.python.org)

Find recent blog posts about a topic that mention a specific phrase:

intitle:"python projects" intext:"for beginners" after:2025-01-01

Search inside a single documentation site:

site:pandas.pydata.org merge dataframe

Find social profiles for a person (works across most major platforms):

"Jane Smith" (site:linkedin.com OR site:github.com OR site:twitter.com)

Operators Google has quietly dropped

Two operators that used to be on every "advanced search" cheat sheet don't work the same way in 2026:

  • link: — used to find pages linking to a URL. Now mostly broken; use a backlink tool instead.
  • info: — used to show what Google knew about a URL. Removed from public search in 2023.

When to stop using operators

If your search has more than three operators, you probably want a different tool. Use Perplexity for research with citations, grep.app for searching open-source code, or a dedicated documentation search for SDK references. Operators are for the in-between cases — when Google is the right tool but you need to push it harder than the average search.
